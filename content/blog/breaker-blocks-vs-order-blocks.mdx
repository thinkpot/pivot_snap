Order blocks and breaker blocks get confused constantly, and it's easy to see why — a breaker block starts its life as an order block. The difference only shows up after the fact, once structure has broken. This guide lays out exactly how each one forms, what separates them, and — more importantly — when to trade one over the other.

## Order Block vs. Breaker Block: The Short Answer

An **order block** is a continuation tool. It's the last opposing candle before a strong, structure-breaking move, and traders expect price to retrace to it and then continue in the original direction.

A **breaker block** is a reversal tool. It's an order block that already failed once — price broke through the structure it was defending — and it now flips roles: what used to hold price back becomes the level that pushes it the other way.

Same origin, opposite job. That's the whole distinction in one sentence, but the details of how and why matter for actually trading either one well.

## What Is an Order Block?

An order block is a price zone — typically the last candle moving against the dominant direction right before an impulsive, structure-breaking move. The logic: large participants (institutions, funds, anyone trading in size) can't fill enormous orders in one candle without moving the market against themselves, so they build positions gradually. The last candle before price finally breaks away sharply is thought to mark where a meaningful chunk of that positioning happened.

**Bullish order block:** the last down-close candle before a strong rally that breaks a swing high.

**Bearish order block:** the last up-close candle before a strong decline that breaks a swing low.

Order blocks are a **continuation** concept. The expectation is that price retraces to the order block, finds support (or resistance), and resumes the original trend — not reverses.

## What Is a Breaker Block?

A breaker block is what happens when an order block fails. The sequence looks like this:

1. Price forms a swing high or swing low.
2. An order block forms and an impulsive move breaks that swing point, as expected.
3. Instead of continuing, price reverses and breaks back through the swing point in the opposite direction.
4. The original order block — having failed to hold as a continuation zone — is now called a breaker block, and it's expected to act with the opposite role on the next retest.

A **bullish breaker** forms after a swing low breaks down, then price reverses and breaks back above the prior swing high — the last down-close candle before that reversal becomes support on a retest.

A **bearish breaker** forms the mirror way: a swing high breaks up, price reverses down through the prior swing low, and the last up-close candle before the reversal becomes resistance.

The key giveaway: a breaker block has already been "tested and failed" once. That prior failure is exactly why traders treat the retest as a stronger flip zone than an ordinary order block that's never been challenged.

## Side-by-Side: Order Block vs. Breaker Block

- **What it marks:** an order block is the last opposing candle before an impulsive move; a breaker block is a failed order block, after a reversal.
- **Expected role:** an order block is a continuation zone — price resumes the original trend; a breaker block is a reversal zone — price flips direction.
- **Requires a structure break to exist?** An order block doesn't — it forms before the breakout candle even happens. A breaker block does — it only exists after structure breaks and then reverses.
- **Best market condition:** order blocks suit steady, trending markets with repeated retests; breaker blocks suit a clear liquidity grab or momentum shift.
- **Reliability driver:** an order block's reliability comes from the volume/impulse behind the original move; a breaker block's comes from how decisively the original level failed.

## How to Identify Each on a Chart

**For an order block:**
1. Find a strong impulsive move that breaks a recent swing high or low.
2. Locate the last opposing candle immediately before that move.
3. Mark its full range (high to low, or body only, depending on your method — just stay consistent).
4. Watch for price to retrace into that zone and continue in the original direction.

**For a breaker block:**
1. Start the same way — find the order block and the structure break it caused.
2. Watch what happens after: does price reverse and break back through the opposing swing point?
3. If yes, that original order block is now a breaker block.
4. Wait for price to return to that same zone and look for a reaction in the new (opposite) direction.

## Trading Order Blocks

**Entry:** Wait for price to retrace into the order block and show signs of holding — a rejection candle or a resumption of the original momentum.

**Stop loss:** Beyond the far edge of the order block zone.

**Target:** The next logical structural level in the direction of the original trend.

**Best used when:** the broader trend is clear and you're looking for a lower-risk entry into an existing move, rather than trying to catch a reversal.

## Trading Breaker Blocks

**Entry:** Wait for price to return to the breaker zone and react — don't enter on the first touch without confirmation.

**Stop loss:** Beyond the far edge of the breaker block, giving the zone room to be tested without invalidating the setup on a minor wick.

**Target:** The most recent opposing swing point, since that's the level the market needs to reclaim for the reversal thesis to keep playing out.

**Best used when:** you've already seen a decisive structure break followed by an equally decisive reversal — not on the first sign of exhaustion in a trend that hasn't actually broken yet.

## How They Interact With Other Smart Money Concepts

Order blocks and breaker blocks rarely exist in isolation on a real chart. They frequently line up with:

- **[Fair value gaps](/blog/fair-value-gap-trading)** — an FVG sitting inside or beside an order block or breaker adds confluence to the zone.
- **[Supply and demand zones](/blog/supply-demand-trading-zones)** — the "base" that precedes a rally or drop often overlaps with where an order block forms.
- **[Liquidity zones](/blog/tradingview-indicator-liquidity-zones-for-reversal-entries)** — breakers frequently form right after a liquidity sweep, since that's often what fuels the sharp reversal in the first place.

When two or three of these line up at the same price level, traders generally treat that as a stronger zone than any one concept flagged alone — this is usually called "confluence," and it's a big part of why smart money trading rewards patience over pattern-spotting speed.

## Common Mistakes

- **Labeling every retracement zone an order block.** The move away from it needs to be genuinely impulsive and structure-breaking, not routine price action.
- **Calling a breaker block before the reversal is confirmed.** If price hasn't actually broken back through the opposing swing point, it's still just a failed order block candidate — not a confirmed breaker.
- **Ignoring which one you're actually trading.** Order blocks and breakers call for opposite trade directions at similar-looking zones. Confusing the two is one of the fastest ways to trade the wrong side of a setup.
- **Skipping higher-timeframe context.** Both concepts are meaningfully more reliable when they align with the higher-timeframe trend (for order blocks) or a clear higher-timeframe reversal signal (for breakers).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can an order block become a breaker block later?**
Yes — that's the only way a breaker block forms. Every breaker starts as an order block; it only earns the "breaker" label once the market has proven that level failed and price has reversed back through the structure it was defending.

**Which is more reliable, order blocks or breaker blocks?**
Neither is universally more reliable — they're suited to different market conditions. Order blocks tend to work better in steady trends with repeated retests; breaker blocks tend to work better around clear liquidity grabs and momentum shifts. Trading the wrong one for the current market condition is a more common problem than either concept being inherently weak.

**Do I need both concepts, or can I just focus on one?**
Most traders eventually learn both, since a normal market cycles between trending (where order blocks are more useful) and reversing (where breakers matter more). Focusing on just one leaves you without a framework for the other half of what price actually does.

## Putting It Into Practice

Order blocks and breaker blocks are two sides of the same underlying idea — a price zone where meaningful positioning happened — but they call for opposite expectations depending on whether that zone held or failed.
